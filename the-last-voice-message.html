The Last Voice Message

Some messages are better left unheard.

My brother called me at 11:46 p.m. on a Tuesday.

I know the exact time because I was washing dishes when my phone started vibrating on the kitchen counter. His name appeared on the screen, and for a moment, I just stared at it.

Daniel had been dead for eight months.

I didn't answer.

The call stopped after six rings.

I stood there with my hands dripping wet, trying to convince myself that someone had changed their number or that my phone was displaying an old contact by mistake.

Then a voicemail notification appeared.

I nearly deleted it.

Instead, I sat at the kitchen table and pressed play.

At first, there was only wind and the faint sound of traffic. Then Daniel spoke.

“Hey, Em. It's me.”

I stopped breathing.

His voice sounded exactly as I remembered it. A little tired, slightly amused, like he'd called to ask for a favor and already knew I'd complain about it.

“Listen, I know this is going to sound weird, but I need you to do something for me. Go to Mum's house tomorrow. Check the drawer underneath the stairs. Don't let Dad throw anything away.”

There was a pause.

Then he laughed softly.

“And Em? Don't tell him I called.”

The message ended.

I played it again.

Then again.

By the fourth time, I was crying so hard I could barely hear him.

My brother had died in a car accident on a rainy night last winter. I'd been the last person he'd spoken to before leaving work. We'd argued over something stupid, and I'd ignored his final call because I was angry.

I had spent eight months wishing I could hear his voice one more time.

Now I had.

And somehow, it felt worse than having nothing.

The next morning, I drove to our parents' old house.

Dad had moved into a smaller apartment after Mum died, and the house was being prepared for sale. Most of the furniture had already gone. The rooms looked strangely large without the things we'd grown up with.

I found the drawer beneath the stairs exactly where Daniel had said it would be.

Inside were old batteries, tangled cables, a broken torch, and a small blue envelope with my name written on it.

The handwriting was Daniel's.

I sat on the floor before opening it.

Inside was a photograph of the two of us as children, standing beside the lake where Dad used to take us fishing. On the back, Daniel had written:

For Emily. In case I chicken out.

There was no letter.

Just a tiny brass key taped to the photograph.

I searched the drawer again and found a locked metal box pushed against the back panel. The key fitted.

Inside was Daniel's old phone.

Its screen was cracked, and the battery was completely dead.

I took it home and charged it.

There were hundreds of photographs, old conversations, and voice recordings. Most were ordinary things: him complaining about work, sending me terrible jokes, recording our dad snoring on the sofa.

Then I found a folder dated three days before his accident.

It contained one audio file.

The title was simply: EMILY — LISTEN ALONE.

I hesitated for nearly ten minutes before pressing play.

Daniel's voice came through the speaker, quieter than usual.

“Okay. If you're hearing this, I probably didn't get the chance to tell you in person.”

He took a breath.

“I found something out about the accident. I don't know how to explain it, and honestly, I don't know if I'm right. But there's a reason Dad never talks about that night.”

A chair scraped in the background.

“Remember when we were kids and he told us Mum died at the hospital? That's not exactly what happened.”

I paused the recording.

My mother had died when I was sixteen. Daniel was nineteen. We'd never questioned Dad's version of events because grief had made the details difficult to discuss.

I pressed play again.

“I found her medical records. She wasn't taken to the hospital until hours after the accident. Dad said he was there with her, but the records say nobody brought her in until the following morning.”

Daniel went quiet.

“I'm not accusing him of anything. I just need to know why he lied.”

The recording ended.

I sat on the sofa until the room grew dark.

Then I called Dad.

He answered after the second ring.

“Emily? Everything all right?”

I wanted to ask him a hundred questions, but only one came out.

“What happened the night Mum died?”

The silence on the other end was immediate.

“Where did you hear that?”

I closed my eyes.

“Daniel.”

Dad exhaled slowly.

For a moment, neither of us spoke.

Then he said, “You need to stop looking through his things.”

“Why?”

“Because some things don't get better just because you know the truth.”

He hung up.

That night, another voicemail arrived.

11:46 p.m.

The same number.

I didn't want to listen.

I listened anyway.

“Em, if you've found the phone, you've probably heard the recording. I'm sorry. I should have told you sooner.”

His voice sounded strained, as though he'd been running.

“I went back to the house. There's something behind the old boiler in the basement. I think Dad left it there years ago. Whatever you do, don't let him know you've found it.”

A loud bang interrupted him.

Then Daniel whispered something I had to turn the volume up to hear.

“He knows I'm looking.”

The message ended.

I checked the call details. The number was Daniel's old number, disconnected months ago.

I called it back.

A recorded voice said the number was no longer in service.

I didn't sleep that night.

By morning, I'd convinced myself there had to be an explanation. Maybe the messages had been scheduled. Maybe someone had access to his old phone. Maybe grief was making me connect things that had nothing to do with each other.

I needed answers, even if the answers were ordinary.

So I drove back to the house.

The basement smelled of damp concrete and old paint. I found the boiler behind a stack of cardboard boxes. The space behind it was narrow, barely wide enough for my arm.

My fingers touched something wrapped in plastic.

It was a small digital voice recorder.

I brought it upstairs and sat at the kitchen table.

There was one recording.

The date was the night Mum died.

I pressed play.

At first, I heard the sound of rain and a car engine. Then Mum's voice.

“Please, Peter. The children don't need to know.”

Dad answered, but his voice was muffled.

“They're going to find out eventually.”

“Not if you leave it alone.”

There was a long silence.

Then Mum said something that made my hands go cold.

“Daniel saw you.”

A chair moved.

Dad spoke again, louder this time.

“He was a child. He doesn't understand what he saw.”

“He understands enough.”

The recording crackled.

Then came Daniel's voice, younger and frightened.

“Dad?”

The file ended.

I sat at that table for a long time, listening to the silence in the house.

I didn't know what Daniel had witnessed. I didn't know why Mum had been afraid, or why Dad had hidden the recording.

But I knew one thing.

My brother hadn't been trying to frighten me.

He'd been trying to tell me something.

I called the police and handed over the recorder, the phone, and every message I'd received. They told me they'd investigate the recordings and the inconsistencies in Mum's medical records.

Dad didn't answer any of my calls.

A week later, he sent me a single text.

You were always the one who asked too many questions.

I never replied.

The police investigation took months. I won't pretend I understood every detail, and I won't pretend the truth gave me the peace I'd hoped for. Some answers arrive too late to repair anything.

But I kept Daniel's phone.

I still listen to his old recordings sometimes, especially the silly ones. The ones where he makes fun of my cooking or complains about the weather.

I haven't received another voicemail from his number.

Not once.

Until last Tuesday.

At 11:46 p.m., my phone vibrated on the kitchen counter.

Daniel's name appeared on the screen.

I let it ring.

Six times.

Then the call stopped, and a new voicemail appeared.

I sat down before playing it.

This time, there was no wind, no traffic, no familiar laugh.

Just a few seconds of silence.

Then a man's voice I didn't recognize said:

“Emily, your brother wasn't the only one who saw what happened that night.”

The message ended.

And for the first time since Daniel died, I was afraid to hear his voice again.
