A Psychological Horror Story

The first time I saw the old woman, she was standing at the end of the hallway, holding a cup of tea.

It was nearly three in the morning, and I had just finished checking the locks on the ground floos that looked several sizes too big for her feet.

She watched me for a moment.

Then she smiled and asked, “Has my son arrived yet?”

I assumed she was a guest who had wandered out of her room.

“What's your room number, ma'am?”

She looked down at the cup in her hands.

“Three-oh-nine.”

I remember frowning because our hotel didn't have a room 309.

We had thirty-two rooms in total, spread across two floors. The third floor had been closed for years after a fire damaged part of the building.

I explained this to her as gently as I could.

She didn't argue. She simply turned around and walked toward the staircase.

By the time I followed her, she was gone.

I checked the lobby, the bathrooms, and the locked fire exit.

Nothing.

When I mentioned the incident to my manager the next morning, he stopped stirring his coffee.

“What did she look like?” he asked.

I described her blue nightdress and silver hair.

His expression changed.

“Did she tell you her name?”

“No. Just that she was waiting for her son.”

He stared at me for a few seconds before saying something I still think about whenever I pass an empty hotel corridor.

“Next time you see her, don't ask which room she's staying in.”

Then he picked up his coffee and walked away.

I thought he was trying to frighten me.

I wish he had been.

---

I started working at the Bellweather Hotel six months earlier.

It was an old building on the edge of a coastal town, the kind of place that had once been beautiful but had gradually been forgotten. The carpets smelled faintly of damp, the wallpaper was peeling, and the heating made a terrible knocking sound every winter.

Still, the job paid reasonably well, and the night shifts suited me.

I was saving money to move into my own apartment. Until then, I didn't mind spending eight hours a night behind a reception desk, answering the occasional phone call and watching rain slide down the front windows.

The hotel had a few regular guests, mostly older couples and business travelers who didn't mind the outdated rooms.

Nobody ever asked about the third floor.

At least, not until the Thursday after I saw the woman.

It was raining hard that night. The sort of rain that makes the whole world feel empty after midnight.

At 2:41 a.m., the reception telephone rang.

I picked it up.

“Good evening, Bellweather Hotel.”

For a moment, I heard nothing but static.

Then a woman's voice said, “Could you please bring some fresh towels to room 309?”

I looked at the telephone display.

The call was coming from an internal extension.

309. 

I checked the guest register. There was no room 309 listed.

“I'm sorry, ma'am. Could you tell me your name?”

The woman sighed.

“You people ask so many questions these days.”

I almost laughed.

Then she added, very quietly, “My son will be home soon, and I don't want him to see the mess.”

The line went dead.

I sat there for a while, staring at the phone.

I wanted to tell myself that someone was playing a prank, but there was something about her voice that bothered me. She sounded tired, not frightening. Almost embarrassed.

Like an ordinary person who had been waiting too long for someone who never arrived.

I called my manager.

He didn't answer.

I checked the internal phone system, expecting to find a faulty connection. Instead, the extension list showed something strange.

There were thirty-two rooms on the system.

And one additional extension.

309. 

Its status was marked ACTIVE.

I took a screenshot and sent it to my manager.

His reply arrived almost immediately.

Go home when your shift ends. Don't investigate this.

I read the message twice.

Then I looked toward the staircase.

For just a second, I thought I heard someone slowly walking upstairs.

---

The next afternoon, I asked an older member of staff about the hotel's history.

Her name was Margaret. She had worked there longer than anyone else and knew every leak, broken pipe, and embarrassing story the management preferred to forget.

When I mentioned room 309, she dropped the stack of towels she was carrying.

“Who told you about that room?”

“Nobody. Someone called reception from it.”

Margaret looked toward the manager's office.

“That's impossible.”

“Why?”

She hesitated.

Then she asked whether I had heard about the fire.

I hadn't.

Thirty-one years earlier, a fire had broken out on the third floor of the hotel. Most of the guests escaped, but an elderly woman named Eleanor Vale had been trapped in her room.

Her son, Thomas, had been staying with her that week.

According to the old newspaper reports, he had left the hotel shortly before the fire started.

He never returned.

Eleanor survived the first few minutes of the fire but died before rescuers could reach her.

Her room number had been 309.

Afterward, the hotel permanently closed the third floor. The owners renovated the lower levels and eventually reopened the building, but the damaged rooms were never restored.

“Did they ever find her son?” I asked.

Margaret shook her head.

“He disappeared. Some said he had run away. Others said he never existed.”

“What do you mean?”

She looked uncomfortable.

“Eleanor used to tell everyone that her son was coming home. Even when he was grown, she would wait by the window for him. The staff thought she was lonely.”

Margaret picked up the towels.

“Poor woman spent her final evening setting the table for two.”

I thought about the cup of tea in the woman's hands.

“Was she holding something when they found her?”

Margaret stopped walking.

“What?”

“A cup. Did she have a cup of tea?”

Margaret's face had gone pale.

“The firefighters found an untouched cup of tea on the bedside table.”

She walked away before I could ask anything else.

---

I should have quit that week.

Instead, I convinced myself that I needed proof before I could believe any of it.

I searched the hotel's old records during my break. Most of the documents were uninteresting maintenance reports, but eventually I found a handwritten guest register from the year of the fire.

The entries were faded, and several pages had been damaged by water.

On the page dated November 17, I found Eleanor Vale's name.

Room 309.

Beside it, in a different handwriting, someone had written:

One guest. Two breakfast trays.

I turned the page.

The next morning's register contained a second entry.

Thomas Vale.

Room 309.

Check-in time: 2:41 a.m.

Check-out time: blank.

I checked the date.

November 18.

The day after the fire.

I sat back in my chair.

The register couldn't have been accurate. Thomas had supposedly left before the fire, and the building had been evacuated afterward.

Someone must have added the entry later.

I was about to close the book when I noticed a folded piece of paper tucked into the binding.

It was a short note, written in the same handwriting as the entry.

If he comes back, do not tell him his mother is dead.

I read it several times.

Then I heard a telephone ring upstairs.

Not the reception phone.

A telephone somewhere on the sealed third floor.

I could hear it clearly through the ceiling.

One ring.

Two.

Three.

Then silence.

My computer screen flickered.

The internal phone system had opened by itself.

Extension 309 was calling reception.

I didn't answer.

It rang for nearly a minute before stopping.

A few seconds later, a message appeared on my screen.

MISSED CALL — ROOM 309

Beneath it was another line.

GUEST REQUEST: ONE EXTRA BREAKFAST TRAY.

---

That night, I arrived early and asked my manager to explain what was happening.

He looked exhausted.

For a long time, he said nothing. Then he locked the office door.

“Thirty-one years ago, the hotel owner received a letter from a man claiming to be Thomas Vale,” he said. “The letter arrived a week after the fire.”

“What did it say?”

“He wanted to know whether his mother had suffered.”

My manager rubbed his eyes.

“The owner wrote back. He told Thomas that Eleanor had died peacefully in her sleep.”

“But she died in a fire.”

“Yes.”

“Why would he lie?”

“Because the truth was worse.”

I waited.

My manager finally told me what the old newspaper reports had left out.

Thomas hadn't simply left the hotel before the fire.

He and his mother had argued that evening. He wanted to leave town permanently, and Eleanor had begged him to stay. Neighbors later reported hearing shouting from the third floor.

Thomas walked out at approximately eleven that night.

The fire began less than an hour later.

No one could establish exactly how it started.

The police never charged Thomas with anything. There was no evidence that he had caused the fire, and he had an alibi for part of the evening.

But the hotel owner had discovered something in Eleanor's room afterward.

A letter addressed to her son.

In it, Eleanor wrote that she had decided to stop waiting for him.

She had packed his belongings, returned his letters, and accepted that he might never come home.

The final sentence read:

If you return one day, I hope you will forgive me for not being here.

Thomas never responded to the hotel owner's letter.

According to the records, he died in another country eleven years later.

“He never came back,” my manager said.

“Then who is calling from room 309?”

He looked directly at me.

“I don't know.”

For the first time, I believed him.

---

At 2:41 a.m., the reception phone rang.

I stared at it.

My manager stood beside me, pale and motionless.

The display showed one number.

309. 

He shook his head.

I picked up the receiver.

“Hello?”

A woman's voice answered.

“Is Thomas there?”

I swallowed.

“No, ma'am.”

“He promised he would come home.”

“I know.”

The words came out before I could stop them.

There was a long silence.

Then she asked, “Is it morning yet?”

I looked at the clock.

2:42 a.m.

“Not yet.”

“Oh, good.”

I could hear the faint clinking of crockery.

“I've made breakfast. He likes his eggs soft, you know. He always complained when I cooked them too long.”

Her voice trembled.

“I was cross with him that night. I said things I didn't mean.”

I closed my eyes.

“I'm sure he knew.”

“No,” she whispered. “He didn't.”

Something in the background creaked.

Then she said, “Could you tell him I'm sorry when he arrives?”

The line went dead.

My manager took the receiver from my hand and unplugged the telephone.

Neither of us spoke for several minutes.

Then I noticed something on the reception desk.

A small white envelope.

It hadn't been there before.

My name was written across the front.

Inside was a single photograph.

A young man stood beside an elderly woman in front of the hotel. He looked about twenty-five, with dark hair and a tired smile.

On the back, someone had written:

THOMAS AND ELEANOR VALE — NOVEMBER 17.

Beneath the photograph was a hotel key.

Its brass tag read:

309.

I dropped it.

The key struck the floor, and somewhere far above us, a door slammed shut.

---

I left the Bellweather Hotel before sunrise.

I didn't collect my final pay until a month later, and I refused to go inside the building again.

My manager never contacted me.

Margaret told me that the third-floor telephone system had been disconnected, and the old guest register had been moved into storage.

For a while, I managed to forget about the woman.

Then, nearly a year later, I received a letter.

There was no return address.

Inside was a photocopy of an old newspaper clipping about the fire. Someone had circled a paragraph near the bottom.

It said that Thomas Vale had been cremated abroad, and his ashes had been sent to an address in England.

The address was the Bellweather Hotel.

The ashes had never been collected.

I stared at the page for a long time.

Then I noticed the date printed beneath the article.

November 17.

The same date as the photograph.

That evening, I put the clipping away and tried to watch television.

At 2:41 a.m., my mobile phone rang.

The number was withheld.

I nearly ignored it, but something made me answer.

For a few seconds, I heard nothing.

Then a man's voice spoke.

It was low, tired, and strangely familiar, though I had never heard it before.

“Hello? Is my mother still waiting?”

I couldn't speak.

The man sighed.

“I've been trying to get through for years. They keep telling me the hotel doesn't have a third floor.”

There was a faint sound in the background.

A woman laughing.

Then he said, “Could you tell her I'm sorry?”

The call ended.

I sat in the dark until sunrise.

I have never returned to the Bellweather Hotel, and I don't know whether the old woman is still waiting for her son.

But there is one thing I know for certain.

Every November, on the seventeenth, a photograph appears on the hotel's reception desk.

It shows Eleanor Vale standing beside her son.

And each year, Thomas looks a little older.

Last November, someone sent me a photograph of the desk.

For the first time, Eleanor wasn't looking at the camera.

She was looking toward the staircase.

And Thomas was standing alone in the background, holding a cup of tea.

On the back, in handwriting I recognized immediately, were seven words:

MOTHER, PLEASE DON'T LET ME GO UPSTAIRS.
